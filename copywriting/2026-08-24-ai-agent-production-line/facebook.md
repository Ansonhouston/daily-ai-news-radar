你裝咗成打 AI 工具，點解全公司最忙嗰個，仲係你自己？

我做咗好耐先肯承認：問題唔係我 prompt 寫得唔夠精準。

問題係我從來冇定義過兩件事 —— 上一步做完，點交畀下一步；同埋，今次呢套做法，點樣變成下次可以再用嘅步驟。

所以我表面上請咗一班 AI 員工，實際上每個員工都要我逐個溝通、逐個 train、逐個追進度。工具 A 出咗嘢，我自己搬去工具 B，中間甩漏，最後又係自己執自己改。

連鎖餐廳去到邊間分店味道都差唔多，唔係因為間間都有個天才大廚。係因為每一道工序、每一個標準、邊個位要驗收，一早定義好。

要複製嘅從來唔係一個人，係嗰個人做嘢嘅方法。

於是我砌咗一個「總指揮」。佢唔係自己做曬，佢係先拆步驟，再派畀唔同 agent 去執行，做完佢收貨；有錯漏、未過標準，退返去重做。

同一件事，同 2025 年比，效率快咗接近 20 倍。出街嗰條 Reel 喺 Facebook 跑到 6 萬幾 views。

⚠️ 講清楚：呢個數只可以證明呢套系統真係生產得出一條片，唔代表生產線令條片爆。爆唔爆仲有演算法、出街時機、觀眾反應，好多我控制唔到。

過程入面有三個位，我覺得先係真正嘅分別 👇

**一・AI 第一步唔係創作，係採樣篩選。**
先定搜尋範圍，再設門檻。所謂 outlier 唔係邊條片最多 views，係同個帳號平時嘅表現比高咗幾多倍 —— 通常 5 倍起跳，太多就收窄到 10 倍。系統批量出一張表：播放數、一句講低條片講乜、主要情緒、可唔可以轉成 15 秒短動畫。

咁就唔會見到一條高 views 就當佢係答案。
而我最後揀嘅唔係某條片嘅情節，係「創作條件」：家庭日常、15 秒淨靠動作就睇得明、情緒由平靜升到一個溫暖嘅位。角色、場景、對白全部用香港生活重新設計。

**市場證據唔係創作答案。**

**二・交接要有規格。**
系統會將角色設定、場景、節奏、風險、驗收標準，整理成一份「製作包」。因為如果啲知識只係留喺我個腦、或者留喺上一個 chat，下一步根本唔知要接啲乜。

一句「凌晨爬上張床」唔夠。要寫實角色、床位、秒數、動作、音樂，仲要寫清楚咩一定唔可以出現 —— 例如全片唯一容許出現嘅文字係邊句，因為 AI 生成文字好易出錯。

由一句說話去到真係拍得出，中間差嘅唔係文采，係你可唔可以將導演腦入面嗰啲判斷，全部寫成一份 AI 睇得住嘅規格。

**三・自動化最重要嘅唔係完全冇人，係去到關口一定要有人閂。**
生成影片會燒 credit，所以 AI 要先報數今次要用幾多，批唔批由人決定。出完亦唔算完成，仲要 QC，例如角色有冇突然變樣。真實例子：同一個角色描述連續兩次過唔到平台審核，改咗描述先成功。

Human-in-the-loop 唔係用嚟拖慢自動化。佢係喺 AI 真係燒錢出街之前，留一個煞車。

### FINAL post (link in first comment — recommended)

You've installed a dozen AI tools. So why are you still the busiest person in your own company?

It took me a while to admit the real problem wasn't prompt quality.

I had never defined two things: what one step hands to the next, and how today's approach becomes a repeatable set of steps for next time.

So on paper I had a team of AI workers. In practice I briefed each one separately, trained each one separately, chased each one separately. Tool A produced something, I moved it into Tool B by hand, things got dropped, and I ended up fixing my own output anyway.

A chain restaurant tastes roughly the same at every branch — not because every branch has a brilliant chef, but because every step, every standard, and every checkpoint was defined in advance.

What actually gets copied is never the person. It's the way that person works.

So I built an orchestrator. It doesn't do the work itself. It breaks the job into steps, assigns each step to a different agent, then checks the output. If something is missing or below standard, it sends the step back to be redone.

Compared to 2025, the same job ran roughly 20x faster. The Reel that came out of it did 62K views on Facebook.

⚠️ To be clear: that number only proves the system can produce a video. It does not prove the pipeline made the video take off. Reach still depends on the algorithm, timing, and how people respond — most of which I don't control.

Three parts made the actual difference 👇

**1. The AI's first job isn't creating. It's sampling and filtering.**
Define the search scope, then set a threshold. An outlier isn't the video with the most views — it's the video that outperformed its own account's normal range by a multiple. Usually 5x and up; if too many pass, tighten to 10x. The system returns a table: views, one line on what the video is about, the dominant emotion, and whether it could work as a 15-second animation.

That way a single high-view video never gets mistaken for an answer.

And what I ended up picking wasn't a storyline. It was a set of creative conditions: everyday family life, readable in 15 seconds through action alone, emotion rising from calm to something warm. Characters, setting, and dialogue were then rebuilt around Hong Kong life.

**Market evidence is not a creative answer.**

**2. The handoff needs a spec.**
The system compiles the character setup, setting, pacing, risks, and acceptance criteria into a production package. If that knowledge only lives in my head, or in the previous chat window, the next step has no idea what it's receiving.

"Climbs into bed in the middle of the night" isn't enough. You need the real character, the position on the bed, the seconds, the movement, the music — and an explicit list of what must never appear, like the one line of on-screen text allowed in the entire video, because generated text goes wrong easily.

Getting from a sentence to something actually shootable isn't about better writing. It's about whether you can turn the judgment calls in a director's head into a spec an AI can hold onto.

**3. The point of automation isn't removing every human. It's having a human at the gate.**
Generating video burns credits, so the AI reports the cost first and a person approves it. And output isn't finished on arrival — it still goes through QC, like checking whether a character suddenly changes appearance. Real example: the same character description failed platform review twice in a row, and only went through after it was rewritten.

Human-in-the-loop isn't there to slow automation down. It's the brake you keep before the AI spends real money and ships.

---

But the thing that convinced me this was worth building was something else.

When the system finishes, it goes back and challenges the assumption it started with.

I had assumed the video worked because it had dialogue and zero conflict. The system pulled 12 comparable external videos as a control set. The one with dialogue had over 17 million views, and the one built on comedic conflict scored highest in the whole batch.
