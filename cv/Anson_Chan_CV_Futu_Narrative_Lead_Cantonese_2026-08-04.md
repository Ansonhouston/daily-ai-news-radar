# NARRATIVE CRAFT — MAKING COMPLEXITY FEEL SIMPLE

Your brief: *turning financial complexity into simple, compelling stories — if people don't understand it, they won't trust it.*

That is what I have done weekly for two years, in Cantonese, on a subject people find intimidating and slightly humiliating to admit they don't understand. Money and AI share that property. People do not disengage because a topic is hard. They disengage because nobody told them where to put their hands.

## The method, in four moves

**1. Find the real point of failure, not the obvious one.**
The obvious diagnosis is "the tool is complicated." It almost never is. People stall at the first irreversible-feeling decision — where a wrong choice feels expensive. Writing that opens on the feature loses them; writing that opens on the hesitation keeps them.

**2. Name one enemy, not five.**
One of my stronger pieces reduced a whole category of AI failure to a single culprit and a single fix — *一個全域指令根治AI幻覺問題*. Not a survey of causes. One enemy, one move.

**3. Make the promise structural, and put it in the title.**
The recurring vocabulary across my work is deliberate: **零 Code · 0 基礎 · 一鍵搞掂 · 完整教學 · 附公式範本 · 含 prompt 下載.** Each is a promise about the *shape of the effort*, not the quality of the outcome. "Zero code" is not a feature claim — it removes the reader's main excuse. Where the audience's default state is *this isn't for me*, dismantling that is the copy's first job.

**4. Ship the artifact, not just the explanation.**
Most explanatory work ends at understanding. Mine ends at a downloadable formula — a template, a prompt, a fill-in structure. The artifact is the proof the simplification actually holds. Anyone can make a hard thing sound easy; handing over something that works is a different claim.

## Substantiation, because claims have to survive scrutiny

I don't assert that something is better. I build a test and publish the conditions.

- **13 Hong Kong local scenarios**, head-to-head, to settle which of two competing tools actually won — and the answer contradicted the expected one, which is exactly why the piece worked
- **7 original commercial-grade tests** rather than one flattering demo
- **880-word prompt versus 380-word prompt**, same subject, published difference — one variable isolated so the finding is attributable

This matters more in a regulated category than an unregulated one: claims that arrive with their method attached survive both a skeptical reader and a compliance review. And rigour is usually what makes the work interesting — the tension in *the cheaper version beat the flagship* only exists because the test was real.

## Performance discipline, without going beige

Eight years of paid media means engagement has never been my finish line. I read work down the funnel — **hook rate → CTR → CVR → CPA → ROAS** — and retire creative that wins attention and dies at conversion. But it runs the other way too: the highest-converting work I have made was never the safest. The 0.3x-to-3x turnaround came from moving 70% of budget to cold audiences and rebuilding the creative, not from optimising a winner's headline. Safety was the thing that was failing.

Compliance is the same trade, and I think the same answer holds: a constraint is a brief. "We cannot promise a return" is not a reason for beige copy — it is an instruction to build the feeling somewhere other than the promise.
